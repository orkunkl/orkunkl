The essay opens on a case rather than an idea: a young woman assaulted, no one charged,
and a national outcry that arrived only after a lawsuit two years later. Its question is
what it would mean to be a man in a culture of life, and why that question has become
almost unspeakable — the environmental and social movements oriented toward care,
inclusion and repair have left the archetypal masculine to the manosphere, which was
willing to speak of it and had nothing life-serving to say.

The historical argument runs through Iain McGilchrist's hemispheres: the naming,
categorising, controlling intelligence McGilchrist locates in the left hemisphere
usurping the relational intelligence of the right — the emissary taking the master's
seat. Its material form was agriculture. Surplus in the storehouse and land with borders
had to be defended, so the warrior whose role had been the guardianship of life became
the guardian of property; to secure inheritance, paternity had to be guaranteed, and a
woman's body became the vehicle through which rights passed. The essay's formulation is
that the blade stopped serving the chalice and began guarding the storehouse, and that the
church later sanctified the arrangement by making God only a father.

The essay is as critical of the reaction to patriarchy as of patriarchy. It holds that
the counterculture was co-opted — citing COINTELPRO, and stating as its own belief,
without evidence offered, that the renegotiation of the left was partly deliberate — so
that the structural critique of capital was replaced by identity, and power-over was
inverted rather than dismantled, with shame and guilt as its instruments. A generation of
young men was told that being a man was inherently violent, was offered no ecological
image of the masculine in its place, and withdrew into the nihilism that was recruiting.

Against this it sets an archetypal rather than prescriptive reading: matriarchy is not
patriarchy inverted, because matriarchy centres continuity while patriarchy centres rule.
Its strongest illustration is the Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace, where the grandmothers
chose the leaders, so that any forward action had to account for seven generations behind
and seven ahead — the blade went out into the world, and the chalice decided who carried
it. Charles Eisenstein's myth of the Courtship of Tara supplies the hinge: the World Man
offers to do better, then offers to disappear, and is refused both; he must court Tara and
win consent, and every courtship gift is an act of service to life. Consent is the whole
story, on this reading, and every rape is partly the act of a boy who never came of age.

The closing half is practical, and its claim is that patriarchy severed men from their own
relational capacity as much as it oppressed women. The work described is small and
somatic: catching the dulled, quiet instruments of control — the tone, the silence, taking
up a room — and naming the fear beneath, holding compassion for inherited wounds rather
than blame, treating the earth and sun as teachers of a holding that was never received.
It ends on initiation: a culture without rites of passage produces perpetual adolescents
wielding civilisational-scale power.
