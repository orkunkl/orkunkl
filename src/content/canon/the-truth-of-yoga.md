The claim that structures the book is simple and unwelcome in equal measure: much of what
gets said about yoga is not supported by the texts. The word does not always mean union —
in the Yoga Sūtra, the discipline's most famous text, the aim is described as separation,
isolating consciousness from everything else. And the practice is not five thousand years
old; the earliest evidence points to roughly twenty-five hundred, and while it may be
older, no one can show it.

Simpson's move is context rather than debunking. He teaches at the Oxford Centre for Hindu
Studies and wrote this after a master's in yoga and meditation traditions at SOAS, and his
premise is that the scholarship of recent decades — Mark Singleton on how modern postural
practice took shape, Mallinson and Singleton on the textual sources — is transformative and
inaccessible in the same measure: written for specialists, while practitioners go on
repeating claims nobody has checked.

The form is deliberate. Four parts run from early yoga through the classical period and
hatha to the modern posture boom; each short section takes one element, quotes from
traditional texts, and puts the teaching back in its setting. It can be read straight
through or entered anywhere as a reference, and the stated intention is to stay clear
without oversimplifying — which is why the chapters carry titles like "Imaginary Chakras",
"Whose Sutras?" and "Hidden Inventions".

Its theme is adaptation. Traditions survive by changing, and the difficulty is telling
invention from continuity without either worshipping an imagined origin or deciding that
origin does not matter.
