The Professional Managerial Class is Liu's name for the credentialed salaried stratum —
the people who administer institutions, adjudicate taste, and describe their own position
as a moral achievement rather than an inheritance. Her argument is that this class
converts ordinary competence into superiority: doing ordinary things in a fundamentally
better way, and reading the difference as virtue.

The political consequence is the book's real subject. Because the PMC locates the good in
individual merit, philanthropy and personal consumption, it has a structural interest in
blocking the redistribution that would dissolve its own position — alongside a rhetorical
apparatus for describing that blockage as progress. Taste becomes ethics; consumption
becomes commitment.

It is short, polemical, and in places deliberately unfair to its target. The value is
the clarity of the diagnosis: a class that insists on its own progressive heroism while
administering the machinery it claims to oppose.
