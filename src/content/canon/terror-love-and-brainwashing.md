The mechanism is attachment. A totalist system does not primarily persuade; it takes
control of the relationships through which a person is held together. Isolation removes
the outside attachments, the leader becomes the sole source of both threat and comfort,
and the resulting pattern — disorganised attachment — leaves a person unable to act in
their own survival interests. Terror and "love" are administered by the same hand, which
is what makes the bond so resistant to argument.

The ideological half mirrors the structural half. An all-encompassing structure produces
an all-or-nothing belief system, so reading the ideology tells you what kind of
relationships it is holding in place. Stein draws the same analysis across small
political cults, religious groups, terrorist organisations, totalitarian states and the
most controlling forms of domestic abuse: the difference is scale, not mechanism.

The book is built on the author's own ten years inside a political cult, and it ends
with prevention rather than pathology — open, non-isolating networks are the protection;
closed belief systems are the warning sign. The second edition (2021) extends the frame
to contemporary radicalisation and adds the NXIVM case.
