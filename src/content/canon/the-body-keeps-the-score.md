The argument is that traumatic stress is not a memory problem but a body problem.
Van der Kolk, who spent three decades treating survivors, traces how overwhelming
events leave the stress response recalibrated: the alarm stays on, the body stops
registering its own signals, and the parts of the brain that sequence experience go
quiet. What remains is not a story but a set of reactions — flinching, numbing,
dissociation, a vigilance that outlives the danger that produced it.

The clinical claim is that treatments which only talk cannot reach what was stored
before or beneath language, which is why the second half of the book works through
yoga, theatre, neurofeedback, EMDR and limbic-system approaches. Its insistence that
the same relationship can both wound and repair — attachment as the mechanism of harm
and of healing — is what carried it into wider circulation.

The reception is not uniform. Researchers have criticised the book for citing studies
that do not support its conclusions, for leaning on outmoded material, and for steering
readers away from well-evidenced treatments such as cognitive behavioural therapy. It
is a synthesis written by a clinician with a thesis, and it reads as one.
