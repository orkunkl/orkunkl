This is the write-up of the first **Genesis Experience** — two days with fifty people at
Commons Hub Brussels in January 2025 — convened by Timothée Brès with Pamela von
Sabljar. Genesis began from a question Brès had been circling since 2014: how communities
and decentralised organisations grow without compromising what they are for. He kept
finding that the answer routed through founders, and that the founder question is also a
polarity question.

The frame is **Logos and Eros** — structure and flow — and the emblem they chose is a
horizontal DNA strand, the two wound together. Eros here is not eros as sex. It is the
life-affirming current von Sabljar describes running through soil, trees, and human
bonds. The claim is that leadership which excludes it goes brittle, and that what looks
like a soft topic is actually a design constraint.

The most useful part is the five principles Brès distils for a **generative culture**:

1. Deep listening requires clear boundaries — genuine openness depends on being able to
   say no
2. Creative authority is not aggression — decisive leadership can come from attunement
   rather than control
3. Organisations resist the creativity they claim to desire, especially when it threatens
   established power
4. Eros and Source are complementary — Eros animates emergence, Source gives it direction
   and stewardship
5. Differentiation and emergence are integrated, not traded off

There is a second set for generative leadership itself, stranger and better than the name
suggests: life force over repair, so you stop patching what is broken and tap the current
running through it; *generative brutality*, which cuts whatever constrains vitality to
serve the whole, with death as an ally in the cycle of creation; and a cyclical rhythm
that refuses perpetual spring, alternating bold action with rest.

The line I keep is von Sabljar's: *"The way to come closer together is to start by
honoring the differences."* It is the opposite of the usual move, where closeness is
asked for as the price of setting differences aside — and it is why I read this next to
Nonviolent Communication. Both are trying to build a channel between people that does not
require either side to disappear.

What I respect most is the refusal to pick a side in the culture war it walks into. The
essay is candid that centring sexual difference cost them readers at both ends — too
essentialist for one, too "androgynous" for the other — and argues that the mature
position is to take the signal from each rather than flinch. Cadell Last puts the stakes
plainly: "Cultivating spirituality to go deeper into political economy, getting your
hands dirty with the difficult realities of contemporary life, is the opposite of
bypassing."

They chose a mycelium over an institution, which is the part I am least sure about and
most drawn to.
