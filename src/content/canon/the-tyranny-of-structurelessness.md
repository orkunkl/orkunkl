Freeman's argument begins by refusing the premise. Any group of people that comes
together for any length of time, for any purpose, will structure itself in some fashion.
The choice is never between structure and no structure, only between structures that are
named and structures that are not. Given first as a talk in 1970 and published two years
later under the name Joreen, the essay was written for a movement that had made
leaderless, structureless groups its organisational ideal — and it treats that ideal as a
reaction that hardened into doctrine: a healthy answer to an over-structured society
becoming "a goddess in its own right".

The mechanism is accountability. Where the structure is informal, the rules for how
decisions get made are known only to the people who already know them, so an
unacknowledged elite forms — rarely by design, and often without anyone involved noticing
it has happened. Its power is real but unnamed, which is precisely what makes it
uncontestable: nobody can be answerable for a decision that was never formally taken. The
elite is constituted by friendship and proximity rather than by any claim to authority, so
the group's politics and its social life fuse, and access to its actual business runs
through personal networks.

Freeman is careful that this is a structural observation rather than an accusation. The
informal elite is not a conspiracy; it is what the group's operating mode reliably
produces.

The essay closes with a remedy rather than a lament: authority delegated by name for
specific tasks, accountability back to the group that delegated it, distribution of
authority as widely as possible, rotation of roles, information diffused rather than held,
and equal access to resources. The point is not to abolish leadership but to make it
answerable.
