Subtitled *How to Survive and Thrive During the Collapse of the Welfare State*, and
built on one argument: the logic of violence is what has always decided how societies
are organised, and microprocessing is about to break it. When information can move
without being seized, the state loses its grip on the things it taxes — and the
individual stops being an asset on someone's balance sheet.

The book's most quoted line is about money: cybermoney will be controlled by private
markets, its importance carried by mathematical algorithms with no physical existence.
That was written in 1997. The rest of the forecast is less comfortable, and they say
so — governments that can no longer tax will not shrug. They will get worse: hostage-
taking as withholding tax, sabotage of the technologies that let wealth escape.

What earns it a shelf here is the megapolitics. It doesn't argue that decentralisation
is desirable, it argues it's what the physics of the situation produces. That is the
same claim the protocols I work on are making, twelve years later, in code rather
than prose.
