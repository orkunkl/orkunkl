This is Girard's fullest statement, and unusually for a work of theory it arrives as a
conversation — a long dialogue with the psychiatrists Jean-Michel Oughourlian and Guy
Lefort, who press him until the arguments have to hold. It appeared in French in 1978
and in English in 1987. The title is from Matthew: *I will proclaim what has been hidden
since the foundation*.

The mechanism he claims to have uncovered runs like this. Desire is not spontaneous —
it is **mimetic**. We desire what a model desires, and the model who wants the same
object is also the obstacle to it. This is where the theory earns its keep: rivals in
that position do not diverge, they *converge*. Both hands reach for the same thing and
become more alike in the reaching, which is exactly what each was trying to avoid. He is
careful to say competition is productive while it stays inside limits; past them,
"rivals are more apt to forget about whatever objects are the cause of the rivalry and
instead become more fascinated with one another."

Pushed far enough, mimetic rivalry dissolves every difference in a society at once. That
is the **mimetic crisis** — indiscriminate violence with no structure left to contain
it. Girard's claim is that such crises were resolved by the **scapegoat**: the group
turns as one on an arbitrary victim, and the murder works. It discharges the violence,
restores unanimity, and founds the social order. All are one against the rejected other.
Because the community cannot bear to know what it has done, it misremembers the event as
the act of a god. On this reading the sacred is our own violence misrecognised, and
ritual sacrifice is the mechanism repeated at the lowest available cost.

What makes it more than a theory of archaic religion is the last move. Girard argues the
Judeo-Christian texts do not join the misrecognition — they expose it, by taking the
victim's side. Against Freud's *Totem and Taboo* he insists Christianity resembles
archaic religion in form and inverts it in meaning: if Christ is sacrificed, it is not
because God willed it but because human beings wanted it, and the texts say so.

I read it beside *Meditations on Moloch* because the two describe the same trap from
opposite ends. Moloch has no exit built in — imitation escalates and nobody can stop.
Girard's answer is that societies did find an exit, and that it was a killing they then
had to forget. That is a darker claim than the coordination literature usually permits,
and I have not found a good counter to it.
