The claim is that a vocabulary born of real suffering has been repurposed. Liu traces the
line from shell shock to PTSD, through the therapeutic turn of the late twentieth century
and the conversion of pain into broadcast capital, to an authenticity that now functions
as a brand. What begins as a clinical description of injury becomes a form of identity,
and then a form of currency.

The political argument follows from that. A public life organised around the recognition
of wounds substitutes emotional display for action, and enforced accommodation for
challenge. The solidarity that could confront whatever produced the harm dissolves into
competing claims of suffering — a politics of false consensus that ends by defending
nobody.

The book is a polemic and reads as one. Its target is the use of trauma as authority
inside liberal professional culture, not the reality of traumatic injury; that
distinction is the whole argument, and readers who collapse it will misread the book.
