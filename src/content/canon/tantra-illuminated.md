Wallis wrote this as an introduction to a tradition that began about 1,500 years ago in
the far north of India, and which the West encountered a century ago mostly through
misinformation. His opening move is about naming: the English word for this tradition
arrived carrying a hundred years of speculation and fantasy, while the tradition's own
definition of itself is precise and almost entirely philosophical.

The book moves in three parts.

**The philosophy.** Non-dual Śaiva thought, approached through its own categories — the
claim that the structure of reality is not something added to consciousness but
consciousness's own self-presentation, and that the world is therefore not a fall away
from the absolute but its expression.

**The history.** Early Śaiva lineages and the two streams they divided into, the nine main
sects, the shape of the canon, the great eleventh-century synthesis around Abhinavagupta,
the Kaula lineages, and the long post-classical descent. One of its sharper arguments is
that modern postural yoga is a late descendant of these lineages rather than a continuous
thread from antiquity.

**The practice.** The descent of grace and initiation, the role of the guru, the three
means to liberation — divine, empowered, and individual — and the theory and structure of
ritual, ending with what undertaking a sādhana looks like in the modern world.

Wallis holds a Berkeley doctorate in Sanskrit and a traditional initiation, and the book
is built from primary texts rather than summaries. What holds it together is that he
treats the tradition as a tradition: with an internal argument, a history of disagreement,
and a practice that assumes the philosophy rather than merely illustrating it.
