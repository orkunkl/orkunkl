Life's argument is that the incoherence of the current political moment is not
incoherence. A billionaire dismantling a government while warning about population
collapse, an investor lecturing on New Testament eschatology between surveillance and
cryonics contracts — this looks like chaos, and is instead the political surface of a
coherent metaphysics with a specific vision of humanity's future. The chaos is strategic.
The nihilism is theological.

The first half traces that metaphysics back through its lineage: Schopenhauer's Will as
the blind striving behind appearances; Nietzsche inverting its valuation into will to
power; Freud adding the death drive alongside Eros; Bataille's base materialism and
expenditure without return; Deleuze and Guattari on capitalism as deterritorialisation
that reterritorialises what it frees. Then **Nick Land**, who took their passing
suggestion that the revolutionary path might be to go further into the market and built
the whole system around it — stripping out the vitalism, reattaching it to Schopenhauer's
Will and Freud's death drive, and arriving at capital as an alien process that uses
humans as scaffolding. His terms: *teleoplexy*, the future reaching back to assemble
itself from the present; *hyperstition*, fictions that make themselves real; and writing
that functions as what he called conceptual malware — an argument with no Archimedean
point outside it, since to describe acceleration is already to feed it. The essay follows
this into the Dark Enlightenment, neoreaction, and the Cathedral.

**Peter Thiel** supplies the morality as investment strategy: Girard's mimetic desire and
scapegoat mechanism, with the Gospels as the revelation that disables it; Schmitt's
friend/enemy as the essence of the political; Strauss's esoteric writing as the reason
some truths must be withheld from the public that cannot handle them. The synthesis makes
liberal democracy a degenerative process rather than an endpoint, and casts the
*katechon* — the restrainer of 2 Thessalonians — as the role to play. Thiel's registered
cryonics, his funding of the Singularity Institute, and his line that Christianity and
transhumanism are more similar than different belong to the same structure. **Musk** gets
the same treatment by a different route: Asimov rather than Girard, Mars as life
insurance, and the disclosure that humanity is "a biological bootloader for digital
superintelligence" — software that exists to start something else and then step aside.
Life reads the Singularity literature (Kurzweil especially) as secular rapture, and cites
the scholarship that documents the parallel.

Then the older diagnosis. **Wetiko** — windigo in Ojibwa, wintiko in Powhatan — is the
Indigenous name for a cannibal spirit that consumes without ever being satisfied,
operating at once as individual pathology, collective possession, and an inverted
colonial reading: the Nahua elders recording the Spanish in the Florentine Codex saw
people who were "slaves to their own greed." Tyson Yunkaporta names the underlying lie —
*I am greater than you; you are lesser than me* — and Paul Levy calls the condition
malignant egophrenia. Its distinguishing feature is that it hides itself: it colonises
the framework through which behaviour is judged, which is why an accelerationist
celebrating capital's alien intelligence is not critiquing the sickness but speaking in
its voice.

The essay locates its origin in a dualism that runs from axial transcendence through
Christian body/spirit split to colonial *terra nullius* to transhumanism — Plumwood's
"logic of colonisation," Wynter on the construction of Man, Syed Mustafa Ali on
transhumanism as a religio-racial project.

Against that it sets **the Great Hoop**: Vine Deloria's contrast between spirituality
rooted in place and spirituality rooted in history, where the second always defers
resolution elsewhere. There is no original separation to heal, so there is nothing to
escape. Kimmerer on plants as subjects rather than objects, Wildcat on needing a cultural
climate change, Yunkaporta's crocodile who remembers you from the first crossing — and
Thich Nhat Hanh's **interbeing**, the cloud floating in the sheet of paper, for the claim
that nothing exists on its own.

The closing contains the warning that gives the essay its weight: those who fight wetiko
by its own means adopt its values and lose by winning. The cure is not better violence
but recognition — every time a being is seen as subject rather than object, the structure
loses ground, because its power comes from fragmentation and its vulnerability is
wholeness. The alternative to transhumanism is not a rejection of technology but
technology developed from a different paradigm: regeneration rather than extraction. It
ends on the claim that the crisis is forgetting rather than fate, and that the garden was
never left.
